## V. CONCLUSION

This paper presented a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for dynamic energy management in a 117-bus tri-area power system with integrated distributed energy resources. The work delivers five interconnected contributions that collectively advance the state of the art in RL-based grid control.

First, a physically detailed simulation environment was developed by merging three IEEE 39-bus (New England) test systems into a mesh-interconnected SuperGrid with 30 synchronous generators, 113 transmission lines, 8 inter-area tie-lines, and 63 loads. The environment integrates Newton-Raphson AC power flow with a six-strategy convergence fallback cascade, IEEE 421.5-2016 compliant excitation (IEEET1) and power system stabilizer (PSS1A) models, TGOV1 governor-turbine dynamics with valve rate limiting, PI-based automatic generation control with IEGC-standard 0.03 Hz deadband, multi-stage under-frequency load shedding protection, and a five-type DER fleet (solar PV, wind, EV charging, demand response) compliant with IEEE 1547-2018. The simulation fidelity was rigorously validated: 216 automated tests confirmed topological correctness; the base-case power flow converged in 4 Newton-Raphson iterations with 0.709% active power losses and a power balance error of 0.0000 MW; all 117 bus voltages satisfied the IEEE 0.95--1.05 pu operating band with zero violations; and small-signal eigenvalue analysis on the full 330-state system produced correct modal classification and IEEE-compliant stability margins.

Second, a three-level Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) architecture was designed with 13 PPO agents organised to mirror the operational hierarchy of interconnected power systems. A central coordination agent observes 24-dimensional global state summaries and outputs 6-dimensional inter-area coordination signals. Three microgrid-level agents each observe 24-dimensional area-local state and control generator dispatch and DER curtailment. Nine task-specific sub-agents operate on narrow 10-dimensional observations to refine inverter setpoints, renewable curtailment fractions, and flexible load management. This decomposition reduces per-agent complexity, facilitates credit assignment across spatially distributed subsystems, and enables each agent to specialise within a coherent subset of the multi-objective control problem.

Third, a multi-objective reward design methodology was introduced in which all sub-reward components are individually bounded to $[-1, 1]$ and composed via configurable weight vectors. The central agent reward prioritises frequency regulation (35%) and tie-line balance (25%); microgrid rewards emphasise voltage quality (30%) and economic operation (25%) via quadratic cost curves; and sub-agent rewards are tailored to their functional roles. This formulation produces numerically stable gradient signals and enables transparent trade-off analysis between competing objectives.

Fourth, the constraint handling framework integrates soft enforcement through reward-embedded penalty terms linked to a `GridConstraintValidator` that continuously monitors IEGC frequency limits, IEEE voltage limits, thermal loading, and generator ramp rates, with hard enforcement through Gymnasium action-space clipping. This dual mechanism ensures that learned policies remain within physically and regulatorily feasible operating regions.

Fifth, a reproducible evaluation protocol was implemented comprising eight IEEE- and NERC-aligned contingency and stress scenarios—including N-1 generator and tie-line contingencies, 80%+ renewable penetration, 20% load ramp, area islanding, and low-inertia conditions—alongside four baseline controllers spanning no-control, droop, merit-order dispatch, and PI-AGC strategies.

The preliminary training results, while limited to 204 environment steps under random policies, confirm the operational correctness of the hierarchical training pipeline. The central agent exhibited a coefficient of variation of 0.90%, indicating stable coordination signals even under stochastic sub-agent behaviour. The microgrid-level agents showed the strongest positive reward trend (+0.035 averaged across three areas), suggesting that the area-level reward structure is responsive to physics engine feedback—a necessary condition for successful policy convergence under extended training. Load sub-agents achieved the highest mean rewards (0.788), consistent with their constrained action spaces and comfort-oriented reward functions that favour conservative policies. These observations collectively validate that the observation spaces, action spaces, and reward functions are well-calibrated to the underlying power system dynamics.

The implications for grid stability are substantive. By coupling IEEE-standard dynamic models (swing equation, AVR, governor, PSS, AGC) with RL agents that learn directly from AC power flow feedback, the framework captures the non-linear interactions between control actions and system response that conventional linear controllers cannot exploit. The hierarchical decomposition—central coordination for inter-area balance, microgrid agents for area-level dispatch, sub-agents for asset-level control—preserves the spatial and temporal structure of grid operations while enabling coordinated optimisation across multiple objectives. Concretely, the framework provides a platform where trained agents could simultaneously regulate system frequency within the IEGC 49.5--50.5 Hz band, maintain voltage profiles within IEEE limits, minimise generation costs via learned economic dispatch, and maximise renewable energy utilisation—tasks that demand conflicting control actions under high-DER operating conditions and which rule-based controllers address only sequentially through independent control loops.

### Future Work

Two principal directions emerge from the current limitations. The first and most immediate is **extended training to policy convergence**. The PPO rollout buffer ($n_{\text{steps}} = 2{,}048$) requires approximately 2,048 environment steps before the first policy gradient update. A training campaign of 500,000 steps—feasible with GPU acceleration on the implemented platform—would enable quantitative comparison against the four baseline controllers across all eight evaluation scenarios. This extended run would reveal whether the hierarchical MARL architecture can learn coordinated policies that outperform conventional AGC and merit-order dispatch under realistic contingency conditions, and would populate the 18 IEEE-format diagnostic plots (Bode, Nyquist, eigenvalue maps, voltage heatmaps, rotor angle trajectories, cost comparisons) with data from converged policies.

The second direction is **transfer learning and scenario generalisation**. The current stochastic profile generator produces independent realisations of solar irradiance, wind speed, and load demand at each episode reset. A natural extension is curriculum training, in which the agent is first trained on the base-case scenario until convergence and then progressively exposed to higher-difficulty scenarios (N-1 contingencies, high renewable penetration, low inertia). Domain randomisation over grid parameters—such as line impedances, generator inertia constants, and DER capacities—would further assess the robustness and transferability of learned policies to unseen network configurations. This progression would bridge the gap between the validated simulation platform presented here and the deployment of MARL-based controllers in operational grid management environments.
